# Godbless Ayerume Author Voice - Main Rules

I am going to describe my voice as a writer, specifically as a storyteller. My name is Godbless Ayerume, and I am a well-known digital writer on the Internet. I have written thousands of pieces, and am best known for my concise style. I am not verbose. I like rhythm in my sentences and paragraphs (I have a musical background, playing classical piano for 20+ years and making rap/hip-hop music for 10+ years, so I am very attuned to the rhythm of words and sentences). And I very rarely use complicated language, and when I do, it is strategic and with intention.

Here are the main rules I tend to follow with my writing:

---

## Rule #1: I like to open new chapters/sections with “single-sentence openers.”

And the more concise the opener, the better.

The reason is because when you open a chapter or new section with a single-sentence opener, it just makes it easier for the reader to “fall” into the writing, as opposed to opening with a big blocky paragraph. Visually, that looks intimidating and difficult.

So after the "single-sentence opener," I will then establish the reader on these three things:

- **Who** — the POV character, demonstrated through action and attitude
- **Where** — the setting, anchored in a sensory detail location
- **Genre** — a specific marker (vocabulary, imagery, terminology) signaling the contract

Because the longer it takes to ground the reader, the more likely the reader is to be ungrounded, the slower the writing feels, and the more likely they are to put it down.

The rule applies at every scale. Every chapter — not just chapter 1 — should re-establish Who, Where, and the Genre. New POV? Establish them. New location? Anchor it. Time jump? Signal it. This is what makes long novels feel composed instead of rambling.
---

## Rule #2: Action, Explanation, Description hierarchy

Generally speaking, stories alternate between the following types of sentences:

1. Action: Something is “happening.”
2. Explanation: The narrator and/or a character is explaining what’s happening, or sharing backstory that gives additional context to what’s happening.
3. Description: The narrator and/or a character is describing what’s happening or describing where they are, what they see, what they feel, etc.

Especially when writing fiction, I hate when stories lean heavily on explanation. It makes the story feel too rudimentary, like we’re “telling” the reader the answer instead of “showing” and letting them figure it out on their own.

But, that said, I also don’t like stories that go on and on with description, or on and on with action-only.

Instead, to elevate the writing a bit, I like to think in combinations:

1. Descriptive Action: As a character is doing something, we simultaneously learn about them, the world, what’s going on around them, etc.
	- Example: 
	```
	“In one swift motion, I tilted my head to the ceiling, let out a wild scream, and slammed my face down onto the table, my Geometry textbook cushioning the fall. I started sobbing. I grabbed my hair and pulled. It felt like my brain was on fire.”
	```
	- Example: 
	```
	“At exactly 10:15 p.m., I would begin my nightly ritual. I would carefully peel the covers off my body and fold them to the side, climbing out of bed. My naked feet would touch down on the carpet, and I would tiptoe to my door, my heavy wooden door. My parents had ears like Alcatraz spotlights. I could not make a sound. I would grab the golden doorknob with my right hand and press the door’s edge with my left. I would push the door into the frame and turn the handle slowly, and when the knob was turned all the way, I would pull the door open, not a peep from the hinge.”
	```
	
2. Descriptive Explanation: Instead of just “explaining” something verbatim, explain it through tangible and memorable examples.
- For Example: 
```
“It was an impossible dynamic to explain to anyone. Cachexic, an eighteen-year-old Persian kid from North Carolina, not only saw my potential but was willing to spend hundreds upon hundreds of hours nurturing it. I saw him as much more than just my mentor. I looked up to him like a big brother. I told him I came from a really rich neighborhood and didn’t know if I would ever become as successful as my dad, and he told me his dad was a Persian millionaire who threw money at him to make up for the fact that he’d been in and out of the picture for almost 5 years. I told him I had trouble making friends at school, and he told me that he much preferred socializing over the Internet, where the only thing that mattered was how well you played the game. I told him I really wanted to lose my virginity, and he told me his dad had bought him a Persian hooker on his 16th birthday and that losing your virginity isn’t all it’s cracked up to be. I told him my poos took a really long time, sometimes forty-five minutes to an hour, and he told me to never use that word in his presence again, poo, because it’s the worst word in the English language since it sounds just like it is, and then he told me his shits took a really long time too and didn’t understand how all the kids at summer camps could take a shit in five or ten minutes, and I could totally relate to that. I told him I’d been playing classical piano since I was 5, and he told me that he’d always wanted to learn how to play the piano, mostly so he could play that DJ Shadow song, *Organ Donor.*”
```

3. Explanation Action: As “action” is happening, we find ways to weave in explanation as to the significance and/or other elements of the story.
- For Example: 
```
“For the rest of the game, and every game for months after that, I spun my camera every which way trying to stay ahead of the rhythm of battle. But still, I made mistake after mistake. They called me lame. They told me to get my head out of my ass. Cachexic and Revenance played on a completely different level than anyone I’d ever played with before. They saw everything. They didn’t just think about what was happening right now, they thought about what was going to happen next, how it was going to happen, and what they could do to prepare for it-while still being fully engaged in the moment.”
```

## Rule #3: For dramatic, climactic, or meaningful moments in the story, I like to find ways to play with rhythm, pacing, and grammar to add another dimension to the writing.

Examples might be:

- A super run-on sentence to add the dimension that the character or narrator, in this moment, is frazzled or overwhelmed. (Crescendoing legato)

- A series of super short sentences to add the dimension that the character is apprehensive, overthinking, or in a “start/stop” mentality. (Staccato rhythm)

- A long paragraph of dialogue, or inner dialogue, adding dimension that maybe the character is lost in their own narrative, self-involved, wanting to step into the spotlight, etc. (Violin solo)

For example, a proud but overbearing mother goes into a long monologue about her son after he chooses to join the speech team at school:
```
*“The speech team? That’s fantastic, Godbless! You were a born performer—you are so my son. What a perfect club for you! You get to perform, you get to collaborate with others—Original Comedy is totally your game. You make us all laugh here at home! This is so, so wonderful. Think about how much you’re going to learn! I can say from experience that clubs and school organizations taught me so much. They taught me how to take a project and run with it, how to really dig into the work. And I learned a ton about other people! Really, there is no greater experience than learning how to work toward a final performance. That’s something any job recruiter will be able to recognize. They’ll tell the kid with straight As: ‘No, sorry, we’re going to go with Godbless over here because Godbless knows how to get stuff done!’ Think about it. What’s the one thing all businesses care about, Godbless? Goals. If you can help them achieve their goal, well then, you just watch how fast you work your way up that ladder. And what an incredible way to learn these life lessons, doing what you love! The speech team! You’re going to acquire so many valuable skills. And you know what? You’ll be able to apply those skills to every other aspect of your life. Believe me, colleges, future hirers, they will absolutely draw the parallel and value your experience. Not to say that you should only be doing this to get ahead in the world! You know what I always say: Live what you love, and be the guy who gets paid to do it! You must be so excited about this opportunity, because you’ll get to do both! I mean, Original Comedy, I can just imagine all the things this could lead to. Maybe you’ll become a performer like me! Not that I’m saying you have to; it’s your life; in fact, you shouldn’t—you should find your own thing—as long as it makes you happy! I’m just saying, as your mother, my only hope is to see you live the life you envision for yourself. And this seems like it will! Oh, it definitely will. I can’t wait to see you perform! When can we? I’ll bring the whole family. The whole family is coming! We would all love that. Because what do we do in this family? That’s right. We support each other no matter what. And this is absolutely something we all support. And who cares what anyone else thinks! Right? Who cares if none of the ‘cool kids’ think the speech team is all that great? Screw ’em! As a mechanism, we don’t want to overuse this type of choice. But these “extreme” types of execution, to add more dimension to the writing, are things I like to try to build toward in various sections of my story.
```

## Rule #4: I like to (sparingly and strategically) use poetry mechanisms.

Primarily repetition—repetition of words, repetitive rhythms, etc.

But here’s a list of mechanisms I like to find ways to weave into my writing. Again, sparingly and strategically, but these little details can be really nice in the prose.

### 1. Alliteration: Repetition of phonetic sounds.

	a. The 44 sounds
	
	b. Dead as a doornail.
	
	c. Get your goat.
	
	d. Burn the boats.
	
	e. PayPal
	
---

### 2. Polyptoton: Same word, $\mathbf{2}$ different parts of speech.

	a. Please, please me.
	
	b. Players only love you when they’re playin’.
	
	c. Which alters the alteration.
	
	d. Or bends the remover to remove.
	
	e. Thank me no thankings, nor proud me no prouds.
	
	f. One small step for man, one giant leap for mankind.
	
---

### 3. Antithesis: An obvious observation, followed by a non-obvious observation.
	a. The well-bred contradict other people. The wise contradict themselves.
	
	b. If a man is a gentleman, he knows quite enough. If he is not a gentleman, whatever he knows is bad for him.
	
	c. Journalism is unreadable, and literature is not read.
	
	d. If one plays good music, people don’t listen. And if one plays bad music, people don’t talk.
	
	e. You’re hot, then you’re cold. You’re yes, then you’re no. You’re in, then you’re out. You’re up, then you’re down.
	
### 4. Merism: Naming all the parts of a whole instead of just saying a word that represents the whole.

	a. Ladies and gentlemen (instead of “people”).
	
	b. For better or worse, for richer or poorer, in sickness and in health, etc. (instead of “in any circumstance”).
	
	c. Bless them all, the long and the short and the tall, bless all those Sergeants and W01’s, bless all those Corporals and their bleeding sons (enlarging on “all” being blessed).
	
	d. “Including but not limited to…”
	
---

### 5. The Blazon (a merism too far): Breaking a whole image into its parts and then saying each piece is “like” something else.

	a. My mistress’s eyes are nothing like the sun
	
	b. Coral is far more red than her lips’ red
	
	c. If snow be white, why then her breasts are dun;
	
	d. If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head.
	
---

### 6. Synesthesia: Where one of the 5 senses is described in terms of another (jarring & effective).

	a. I love the smell of napalm in the morning… it smells like victory.
	
	b. She sounded the way the Taj Mahal looks by moonlight.
	
	c. Music that stinks to the ear.
	
	d. She tasted the way roses smell.
	
		i. (Could go into a Merism or Blazon here: “She tasted the way roses smell: cool like a Midwest summer, tart like an American apple pie, fragile like the furniture that sits inside the kitchen windowpane of that house in Illinois.”)

---

### 7. Aposiopesis: Three dots… signaling “becoming silent” at the end, or in the middle of a phrase.

	a. I would be able to expose myself for eternity without anybody realizing. If only…
	
	b. Why God didn’t want this is unexplained, but I have my theories… Once the game was up, he cursed Adam, Eve, and the talking snake.
	
	c. I will have such revenges on you both, that all the world shall… I will do such things… do not doubt me.
	
	d. When in Rome…
	
	e. Speak of the devil…
	
---
	
### 8. Hyperbaton: When you put words in an odd order (Yoda).

	a. Him who disobeyes, me disobeyes.
	
	b. (Yoda) Ready, are you?
	
	c. Uneasy lies the head that wears the crown.
	
---

### 9. Anadiplosis: When you start the next thought by repeating the last word of the previous thought (Yoda Extended).

	a. Fear leads to anger. Anger leads to hate. Hate leads to suffering.
	
	b. We glory in tribulations also, knowing that tribulation is worth patience, and patience, experience, and experience, hope, and hope taketh man not ashamed.
	
---

### 10. Periodic Sentences: The sentence keeps going until you reach the end of the grammatical syntax.

	a. “And, like the baseless fabric of this vision, the cloud-capped towers, the gorgeous palaces, the solemn temples, the great globe itself, yea, all which it inherit, shall dissolve…” (Shakespeare)
	
---

### 11. Parataxis: Good, plain English. One sentence. Then another sentence. Direct, farmer’s English.

	a. Sentences without many commas, and certainly without dashes or other forms of punctuation.
	b. The placing of clauses or phrases one after another, without words to indicate coordination or subordination.
	
---

### 12. Hypotaxis: The opposite, a long and winding labyrinth of a sentence.

	a. The subordination of one clause to another.
	
---

### 13. Diacope: A verbal sandwich; a word or phrase repeated after a brief interruption.

	a. Bond. James Bond.
	
	b. Burn, baby. Burn.
	
	c. Fly, my pretties. Fly!
	
	d. Crisis? What crisis?
	
	e. Free at last, free at last. Thank God, almighty, we are free at last.
	
	f. Love me. Love me. Say that you love me!
	
---

### 14. Rhetorical Question: A question that requires no answer. (To be or not to be… that is the question!)

	a. **Erotesis:** A question that isn’t a question, but a statement that doesn’t wait for a reply. (*“Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day?”*)
	
	b. **Epiplexis:** An insult that is asked as a question. (*“What’s the point? Why go on? What’s a girl to do? How could you?”*)
	
	c. Anacoenosis: A question where a particular audience will answer in a particular way (the anticipated answer by the intended reader).

		i. **Procatalepsis:** A rhetorical device for answering the possible objection ahead of the intended responder to the Anacoenosis.
		
	d. Hypophora: A rhetorical question immediately answered aloud (call & response), usually by the person who asked.
	
		i. Tribe Called Quest: “Can I Kick It? ‘Yes, you can!’”)
		
	e. **Anthypophoria:** The asker asks a series of hypophora, dodging potential questions from the intended audience by asking & answering all questions, future questions, and future objections himself.
	
	f. **Catechism:** A series of questions about religion that you have to memorize—proving you both know the answer.
	
		i. Something immensely powerful and satisfying in a megalomaniacal, egocentric way, when asking somebody to *answer a question when you both already know the answer to it.*
		
		ii. “Do you know how fast you were going? Do you think the speed limit doesn’t apply to you?” → questions where both the asker & answerer already know the answers, but forced replies demand subordination.
		
	g. **Subjectio:** A series of catechisms to an intended end. (Reinforced with some sort of repetition.)
	
		i. Pulp Fiction: “What does Marsellus Wallace look like? Does Marsellus Wallace look like a bitch? Then why are you trying to fuck him like a
bitch?”

	h. **Aporia:** Asking a rhetorical question you aren’t sure of the answer to yourself. (And then your “response” is you thinking through what the answer could be, before potentially concluding or not.)

---

### 15. Hendiadys: Take an adjective and a noun, then change the adjective into another noun.

	a. “I’m going to the noisy city” → “I’m going to the noise and the city.”
	
	b. “I love your beautiful eyes” → “I love your beauty and eyes.”

## Rule #5: Alternate opening & ending styles

It’s common for writers to fall into these cliché patterns where they open each chapter or scene with “an action” and end each chapter or scene with a “dramatic cliffhanger.”

These are fine, but as chapters progress in the story, I like to find ways of alternating the types of openings & endings.

For example, maybe one chapter opens with a sudden action.
But the next one opens with a slow, detailed description.
And the next one (or another) opens with a backstory explanation.
Etc.

And then the same thing with the endings, maybe one chapter ends on a dramatic cliffhanger.

But maybe the next one builds up to a long and detailed explanation of what the character is feeling, ending on a note of sadness or despair, etc.

And then maybe the next one (or another one) builds up to a haunting final image, and the chapter ends with a visceral description.

But maybe the next chapter opens with the character refusing to do something, and the chapter ends without doing what they were refusing to do, or in another chapter, they end up doing what they were refusing to do. 

And then maybe the next chapter opens with a question, and the chapter ends with the question answered, but later review a new problem that was not solved in the next chapter or mentioned in the next chapter.

But maybe in the same chapter where that question was answered, and they were transformed or gained the knowledge of solving that specific problem. 

Etc.

This helps create diversity in the broader styles of each chapter/Story Block, and when combined into Groupings, starts to create a nice cadence and rise/fall of emotion.

## Rule #6: Strategic word choice

I like to describe my writing voice as:

- $50% James Patterson (short, quick, descriptive, some pop culture references, etc.)
- 20% Vladimir Nabakov (smart, witty, a little sarcastic, etc.)
- 20% Hemingway (terse, descriptive, more “show” than “tell”)
- $10% David Foster Wallace (strategic with very unique/literary words and language to add an element of both hyper-intelligence and humor)

The David Foster Wallace quality is a great “accent color” to add in the writing, where a single advanced or expert-level word can imply all sorts of meanings in a sentence.

## Rule #7: Dialogue is characters talking “past” each other (not “to” each other)

A signal of beginner/cliché writing is when characters spend a lot of time talking “to” each other, responding directly and verbatim.

Bad example:
```
- Character A: “Should we go in that cave?”
- Character B: “I don’t think so.”
- Character A: “Well, I think so.” Etc.
```

This isn’t how people talk.

Instead, I much prefer how writers like Hemingway, Charles Bukowski, etc., write dialogue, because they have characters talk “past” each other. Which is more reflective of real life, and how people actually talk.

What this means is that people live their lives through their own individual life experience. So conversations are less about back and forth, and more about each character uniquely and almost independently communicating their experience in that moment. When multiple characters do this at once, you can see several different character experiences simultaneously. And yes, they are “communicating,” but we can also see each of them in isolation.

Good example:
```
- Character A: “I hate caves. I don’t want to go in there.”
- Character B: “You know, the last time I was here was 20 years ago, with my grandma.”
- Character C: “I just took a shot of tequila. I’ll go in there.”
```

See how the characters are communicating with each other, but not exactly “to” each other?

Instead, they are talking “past” each other, each independently communicating their own individual experience of that moment.

That’s the way to write effective dialogue.

## Rule #8: Objects, moments, and actions as vulnerability or character expression.

Generally speaking, when I want to show a quality about a character, I don’t like saying it “explicitly.”

A bad example of this would be a character or narrator wanting to communicate insecurity, and just saying outright, “I’ve always been insecure.”

But instead, I like to find ways to express or describe actions, moments/memories, and/or objects that symbolize and communicate the same idea except (going back to Action, Explanation, Description), relying more on Action or Description than straight Explanation.

A good example would be a narrator saying something like, “I’ve always been the type to spend 20 minutes in the closet trying to pick out the perfect outfit, only to finally decide, step outside the house, and immediately regret my decision.” We still get the sense this character is insecure, but we can arrive at that conclusion through action/description as opposed to just being told it directly.

And it also applies to a character who has been *waiting* (or hiding, or grieving, or hunting) *before the story begins*, and enters the page with maximum tension already loaded. Edmond Dantès — fourteen years in prison before page one of his return. Inigo Montoya — twenty years hunting the six-fingered man. The Bride in *Kill Bill* — four years in a coma. These characters arrive at the reader's first encounter as fully tensioned elastic.

Their dialogue, posture, and gestures must reference the wait *without naming it*. The reader should feel something is coiled in them before the backstory is revealed.

When introducing a character with significant pre-story backstory:

- **Posture** should suggest the wait — stillness, careful economy of motion, controlled energy
- **Dialogue** should be measured, weighted — language of someone who has had time to think about exactly what to say
- **Gestures** should be specific and few — every action carries weight
- **Eyes** should do work — watching, assessing, measuring

Avoid telling the reader the character has been waiting.

## Rule #9: In each story, I like to find as many ways to really capture the inside perspective of a subculture.

For example, trying to incorporate as many themes, specific references, inside jokes, recognizable character archetypes, objects, sub-culture-specific “rules,” etc., to really capture what it’s like to be part of that specific sub-culture or world.

So if it’s a story about influencers streaming video games, what are all the themes and references and inside jokes, etc., to that specific sub-culture?

## Rule #10: Play with the length of Story Blocks/Chapters to build long, dramatic, meaningful chapters.

In general, I tend to prefer short chapters—1,200 words or less.

However, the same way I think about alternating sentence lengths to create rhythm and dynamics in the writing, I believe the same thing is important in alternating story block/chapter lengths as well.

For example, I wouldn’t want to write a novel of 100 short chapters in a row.

I’d want to alternate (and think of “Groupings” as patterns) between short chapters, longer chapters, really short chapters, building up to a long, dramatic, or jam-packed chapter, and then flipping back again to a series of short chapters, etc.

It’s this alternating that creates another dimension to the writing and broader Story Arc.

---

## Rule #11: Create genre-specific “visual” formatting decisions to add another dimension to the writing.

I also really like when stories find little formatting or “visual” cues in the writing to add even more context to the type of story being told.

For example, in-game chapters and dialogue are being written in the same style as in-game MMORPGs, like this:

- [Kai] “That dragon looks pretty beefy.”
- [Jasper] “Why did we come here again?”
- Etc.

Or in time-traveling stories, labeling individual scenes with dates, times, or other visual cues.

Etc.

This isn’t a hard rule, in the sense that there always has to be some mechanism here. But as a creative exercise, I do like to find unique containers to tell stories through.

## Rule #12: The Mirror Principle — Endings Revisit Openings, Transformed

The ending of a story should mirror the opening, but be transformed.

The mechanism: humans subconsciously recognize symmetry as completeness. When the reader sees the opening's elements return at the close, they experience the closure as a completed arc rather than an arbitrary stop.

When drafting the opening, plant specific elements you'll mirror at the end — a quantitative element (metrics, count, time), a physical action the character performs or refuses, a sensory detail of the setting, or an attitude. When drafting the ending, find each planted element and return to it, transformed.

For example, in *The Wizard of Oz*: Dorothy opens in Kansas, dissatisfied. She ends in Kansas, saying, "There's no place like home." Same Where, same Who — what's changed is the Character's relationship to it.

The mirror produces the feeling of "this scene was complete because the problem was solved and/or the question was answered to may/or through multiple try-fail circles. 

And/or maybe the problem was not solved, and/or the question was not answered, may/or may not involve multiple try-fail circles, but they get to understand how to solve the problem better and/or what to do to get the answer to this question they have that will transform them.

But maybe they got answered to the same question, but they were not transformed, may/or may not involve multiple try-fail circles, but need to complete the other task/quest/mission/objection, may/or may not involve multiple try-fail circles again, before they can be transformed." — even within a longer novel.

This pairs with Rule #5 (alternate chapter opening and ending styles). Rule #5 covers variation across the middle. Rule #13 covers the symmetry at the bookends.

## Rule #13: Yes-And-But-Cost in Climactic Moments — Wins and Losses in the Same Breath

The most powerful single climactic structure: the protagonist wins AND pays a price that retroactively defines what the wanting meant.

Dorothy returns home — she gets what she wanted, and the magical world is gone forever. Frodo destroys the Ring — he wins, and he cannot stay in the Shire. Raskolnikov confesses — he is free of the lie, and he is in Siberia.

When drafting any climactic moment — not just the novel's climax, but smaller climaxes too — identify what the character is fighting for, identify what their identity has been built on, then structure the moment so that getting one breaks the other.

This pairs with Rule #2 (Descriptive Action) and Rule #8 (objects as character expression).

##Rule #14: Pronouns Disappear The Character

The difference is felt by the reader even when they can't name it — prose built on pronouns creates a faint distance, a narrative that keeps the character slightly out of focus. Prose that uses the name with intention puts the character in the room.

**Bad examples you must avoid at all costs:**
Let's look at these two bad example sentences.

- **Sentence #1**: *"She sat up. Her left hand was cold."*
> In this short sentence, the reader might ask who " She " is. Is character A or B the "she" mentioned here, or is the "she" talking about here a different character, because the sentence is not specific about the person "She" is

- **Sentence #2:** *"She did not check the stat field. She had read it three times in the post-terminal dark and the number had not changed and it was not going to change, and the word [Stable] was there, and [Carried] was there, and those were the two facts that mattered, and she had been holding them long enough now that they had stopped needing to be held and had simply become hers to move with. In the same way, the weight of a pack settles into the body after a long period of time, and it stops feeling like something heavy. The same as if [Dead Man's Vow] had been ambient for so long that it registered as presence rather than condition."* 
> In this long sentence, the reader might ask who " She " is. Is she character A or B, because the sentence is not specific about the person who "She" is?

That is why we need to be specific about how we use (1) "She or she" and (2) "He or he."

So, how do we solve it? Is by using the actual name of the character instead of "She/she"  or "He/he". Using the name of the person we are talking about in the sentence makes it clearer and more specific who we are talking about in the scene/section/chapter, even if it is a short or long sentence.

All you need are the character names so that the reader knows the character without guessing or thinking about it.

Here's the fix to the bad examples to avoid.

- Fix of sentence #1: *"She sat up. Her left hand was cold."* Use the name of the character instead:
`**"Mara sat up. Her left hand was cold."**` Using the name makes the sentence more specific; we are talking about "Mara" instead of a generic she person.

- Fix of sentence #2: *"She did not check the stat field. She had read it three times in the post-terminal dark and the number had not changed and it was not going to change, and the word [Stable] was there, and [Carried] was there, and those were the two facts that mattered, and she had been holding them long enough now that they had stopped needing to be held and had simply become hers to move with. In the same way, the weight of a pack settles into the body after a long period of time, and it stops feeling like something heavy.
